💡 What is an Adjective?

"An Adjective is a word which describes or qualifies any Noun or Pronoun."

1. Attributive Use (With Noun)

When an adjective comes directly before a noun to qualify it.

Examples:

  • I met a brave soldier.
  • She purchased an expensive car.

2. Predicative Use (Without Noun / Subject Complement)

When an adjective is used after a linking verb (Be form, seem, appear) as a subject complement.

Examples:

  • He is brave.
  • The soldier is brave.

⚖️ Adjectives vs Determiners (Crucial SSC Distinction - Page 2)

FeatureAdjectivesDeterminers
Complement UsageCan be used as a complement (Predicative)Cannot be used as a complement
GradabilityGradable (Possesses Positive, Comparative, Superlative)Non-gradable (Cannot have degrees)
Adverb QualificationCan be qualified by adverbs (e.g. very brave)Cannot be qualified by adverbs

🌟 The 3 Degrees of Adjective Comparison

1. Positive Degree

Denotes equality or simple quality without comparison.

"You are a talented student."

2. Comparative Degree

Comparison between one vs another (Two entities).

"You are wiser than Rahul."

3. Superlative Degree

Comparison of one vs all (Three or more entities).

"Rahul is the best player of the team."

1. Elder / Eldest vs Older / Oldest & Further vs Farther

Page 3 • Family Relationships vs General Comparison; Additional vs Physical Distance

👥 Elder / Eldest vs Older / Oldest

Elder / Eldest

  • Used strictly for members of the SAME family (blood relations: brothers, sisters, sons, daughters).
  • Used Attributively (before a noun): elder brother, elder sister.
  • When used in comparison, it takes preposition 'to' (never 'than'): He is elder to me.
  • NEVER used for non-living things, animals, or persons outside the family.

Older / Oldest

  • Used for persons outside the family (friends, colleagues, citizens).
  • Used for animals, buildings, monuments, and things (e.g. oldest school, oldest temple).
  • In comparison, it takes the conjunction 'than': He is older than his friend.
  • Can be used both Attributively and Predicatively.
⚠️ Golden Exam Trap (Page 3):
• 2 entities → Comparative Degree (elder / older).
• More than 2 entities → Superlative Degree (eldest / oldest).
"It is difficult to judge which is the elder of the four brothers." ➔ Wrong! Since there are 4 brothers (> 2), use eldest!

📍 Further vs Farther

Further = "Additional / Extra"

Refers to something more in degree, time, thought, or quantity.

Examples:

  • He went abroad for further studies.
  • I need further support / questions for practice.
  • Without any further delay, we started the meeting.

Farther = "Distance"

Refers strictly to physical distance or spatial measurement.

Examples:

  • Mumbai is farther from Patna than Ambala.
  • The library is farther down the street than the bank.

2. Later, Latter, Latest, Last & Few vs Little

Pages 3 & 4 • Time vs Order Dynamics; Countable vs Uncountable Quantifiers

⏱️ The Four-Way Matrix: Later, Latter, Latest, Last

ConceptComparative Form (2 items)Superlative Form (3+ items)Meaning & Usage
TIME (समय) Later (बाद में) Latest (हाल ही में / सबसे नया) Denotes time sequence. Opposite of Earlier / Former.
ORDER (क्रम) Latter (2 में से बाद वाला) Last (सबके अंत में) Denotes position in a list. Opposite of Former (1st of 2) / First (1st of 3+).

Position Rules (Former vs Latter)

  • Between 2 entities (e.g. Rahul & Virat):
    • Rahul = Former (1st of 2)
    • Virat = Latter (2nd of 2)
  • Among 3 or more (e.g. Rahul, Virat & Dhoni):
    • Rahul = First (1st of 3+)
    • Dhoni = Last (Final of 3+)

Late vs Lately

  • Late: देरी से (Opposite of early). "He arrived late."
  • Lately: हाल ही में (Recently). "He hasn't called me lately."
  • Exam Alert: "He will get lately" ❌ ➔ "He will get late" ✅.

⚖️ Few vs Little: Countable vs Uncountable Quantifiers (Page 4)

FEW (Countable Plural Nouns)

  • Few: Hard any / negligible (Negative sense).
  • A few: Some, at least a small number (Positive sense).
  • The few: Whatever small number available, all of them.
  • Comparative: Fewer (e.g. no fewer than 10 men).

LITTLE (Uncountable Singular Nouns)

  • Little: Almost none / negligible (Negative sense).
  • A little: Some, a small amount (Positive sense).
  • The little: Whatever small amount available, all of it.
  • Comparative: Less (e.g. no less than 5 litres of oil).
⚠️ Golden Rule for SSC (No fewer than vs No less than):
• With Countable Nouns (Men, eggs, questions, people) ➔ Use No fewer than.
• With Uncountable Nouns (Milk, oil, money, water) ➔ Use No less than.
• Exception: When a plural number denotes a single unified amount of money, distance, or time, no less than is accepted.

3. Degrees of Comparison — As...As, So...As & Common Traps

Pages 4, 5 & 6 • Structure Alignment, Double Comparative Traps & Intensifiers

📐 Structural Formulas for Comparison

Positive Degree Structure

As + [Positive Adj] + As (Used in both Affirmative & Negative)

So + [Positive Adj] + As (Strictly used in Negative sentences)

"Raman is as brave as his brother."

Comparative Degree Structure

[Comparative Adj / More] + than

Compares two entities or actions.

"He is wiser than you are."

Superlative Degree Structure

The + [Superlative Adj / Most] + of / in

Compares one entity against the whole group.

"She is the most talented student in class."

🚫 Strict Prohibition of Double Comparatives & Double Superlatives:
Never use two comparative or two superlative markers together!
• more wiser ❌ ➔ wiser ✅
• more hotter ❌ ➔ hotter ✅
• most ablest ❌ ➔ ablest or most able ✅
• most noisiest ❌ ➔ noisiest ✅
• more better ❌ ➔ better ✅
• most fastest ❌ ➔ fastest ✅

🔥 Intensifier Rules: Much vs Very

VERY + Positive Degree

Used to intensify base adjectives.

• Very smart ✅ | Very good ✅ | Very cold ✅

• Very better ❌ ➔ Much better ✅

MUCH + Comparative Degree

Used to intensify comparative forms.

• Much smarter ✅ | Much better ✅ | Much faster ✅

• Much good ❌ ➔ Very good ✅

4. Logical Comparison & One vs Group Exclusion

Pages 6 & 7 • That of / Those of, -er vs More for Same Person & Other/Else Rules

🎯 1. Logical Comparison (That of / Those of)

Comparison must always be between similar qualities or entities, never between a person and a quality!

Singular Replacement ➔ 'That of'

Wrong The climate of Delhi is hotter than Mumbai.

Correct The climate of Delhi is hotter than that of Mumbai.

Wrong My bag is costlier than Rahul.

Correct My bag is costlier than that of Rahul (or Rahul's).

Plural Replacement ➔ 'Those of'

Wrong The people of India are more laborious than Pakistan.

Correct The people of India are more laborious than those of Pakistan.

Wrong The works by Wordsworth are more fascinating than Charles Dickens.

Correct ...more fascinating than those by Charles Dickens.

👤 2. Comparing Two Qualities of the SAME Person (-er vs More)

Comparing 2 Different Persons ➔ Use '-er'

When comparing the same quality between two different people.

• Rohit is taller than Mohit.

• Mottappa is taller than Kattappa.

Comparing 2 Qualities of SAME Person ➔ Use 'MORE'

When comparing two different qualities belonging to the exact same individual, never use '-er'; always use MORE + Positive Degree!

• He is wiser than clever ❌ ➔ He is more wise than clever ✅.

• This student is wiser than tall ❌ ➔ He is more wise than tall ✅.

• This hall is wider than high ❌ ➔ It is more wide than high ✅.

🌐 3. One vs Group Exclusion (Concept of 'Other' and 'Else')

When an individual is compared with a group to which they belong, they must be excluded from the comparison pool using 'other' or 'else'.

DegreeExclusion RuleExample
Positive & Comparative MUST EXCLUDE: Use other with any, no, all, most; use else with anybody, nobody, someone. • The Ganga is holier than any other river of India.
• No other animal is as sacred as the cow.
Different Group (No Exclusion) If comparing with a group they do not belong to, do NOT use 'other'! • The Ganga is holier than any river of China. (Ganga is not in China!)
Superlative Degree NEVER USE OTHER/ELSE: In superlative, the subject is already part of the whole group! • Gold is the most precious of all metals. (NOT all other metals ❌)
• He is the cleverest of all students.

5. The 'And' Rule, Gradual Change & Parallel Comparison

Pages 7 & 8 • Degree Harmony across Conjunctions; Continuous Progression & 'The... The...'

🔗 1. The 'AND' Coordinate Rule

When two adjectives are connected by coordinate conjunctions like AND or BUT, they must be in the SAME DEGREE of comparison!

Positive + AND + Positive

He is wise and laborious.

(Not: wiser and laborious ❌)

Comparative + AND + Comparative

Rajat is wiser and more amusing than his wife.

(Both joined by 'than')

Superlative + AND + Superlative

Subhash is the bravest and most energetic person.

(Article 'the' is shared)

⚠️ Shared Article Rule: When two superlatives qualify the same person, the definite article 'The' is placed only before the FIRST adjective:
"You are the best and most talented student" (NOT the best and the most talented).

📈 2. Gradual Change (Continuous Progressive Process)

When an action or quality changes continuously step-by-step over time, use: Comparative Degree + and + Comparative Degree.

• It is getting dark and darker ❌ ➔ It is getting darker and darker ✅.

• You are becoming lazy and lazier ❌ ➔ You are becoming lazier and lazier ✅.

• She is getting more fat and fatter ❌ ➔ She is getting fatter and fatter ✅.

• In his old age, a person gets more weak day by day ❌ ➔ gets weaker and weaker ✅.

• Lives have become healthier and long ❌ ➔ healthier and longer ✅.

⚡ 3. Parallel Comparative: "The... The..." Structure

When two developments or actions are proportionately dependent on each other, use the definite article 'The' with both comparative degrees:

The + Comparative Degree . . . , The + Comparative Degree . . .
  • The higher you go, the cooler it is.
  • The more you work, the more you will get.
  • The faster he completes the work, the larger will be his profit.
  • The more carefully you listen, the more easily you understand.

6. The Selection Rule, Latin -ior Adjectives & Prefer/Preferable

Pages 7, 8 & 9 • The + Comparative of the Two; Latin Adjectives Taking 'To'; Preferable Rules

🏆 1. Comparison vs Selection ('The' with Comparative)

ContextGrammar PatternArticle RuleExample
Direct Comparison Comparative + than NO article before comparative She is better than her friend.
Selection of Two The + Comparative + of the two MUST take 'THE' She is the better of her two friends.
Selection of 3+ The + Superlative + of all / three MUST take 'THE' He was the fastest of the three runners.

🏛️ 2. Latin Comparative Adjectives Ending in -IOR

These 7 adjectives originate from Latin: Superior, Inferior, Senior, Junior, Prior, Anterior, Posterior.

Rules for Latin Adjectives:

  • Always followed by preposition TO (NEVER than!).
  • They already possess comparative meaning, so never add 'more' or 'most'!
  • Can be qualified by much (e.g. much superior to), but NEVER more.

SSC Exam Corrections:

• He is more senior than me ❌ ➔ He is senior to me ✅.

• He came here prior than us ❌ ➔ came here prior to us ✅.

• This cloth is inferior than that ❌ ➔ inferior to that ✅.

☕ 3. Prefer (Verb) vs Preferable (Adjective)

Prefer (Verb)

Takes preposition TO (or rather than with bare infinitives).

• I prefer milk to tea. (NOT than ❌)

Preferable (Adjective)

Already comparative in sense. Takes preposition TO. Never use more!

• Death is more preferable to dishonour ❌ ➔ preferable to ✅.

• Can use much preferable, but NEVER more preferable!

7. Non-Gradable Adjectives, Post-Positive Modifiers & Special Collocations

Pages 8, 9 & 10 • Absolute Qualities, 'Worth Seeing', Redundant Modifiers & 'If Not' Formula

💎 1. Non-Gradable (Absolute) Adjectives

These adjectives represent extreme or absolute states that cannot be graded or increased/decreased in degree.

Unique Perfect Ideal Supreme Universal Excellent Complete Entire Eternal Everlasting Absolute Impossible Round Square Empty Dead Final Chief Extreme
🚫 Absolute Prohibition: Never use more, most, very, comparatively, relatively, -er, -est with non-gradables!
• the most unique ❌ ➔ a unique ✅
• most ideal teacher ❌ ➔ an ideal teacher ✅
• more rounded ❌ ➔ round ✅
• the most perfect curve ❌ ➔ a perfect curve ✅

🎬 2. Post-Positive Adjective: "Worth Seeing / Reading"

Adjectives like worth seeing, worth reading, worth visiting come AFTER the noun they modify, not before!

Wrong a worth seeing movie

Correct a movie worth seeing

Wrong a worth seeing monument

Correct a monument worth seeing

📐 3. Modifiers that Require POSITIVE DEGREE

Use POSITIVE DEGREE with:

Enough, Too, As...as, So...as, Very, Relatively, Comparatively, Half, Twice, Thrice

  • strongest enough ❌ ➔ strong enough ✅
  • too poorest ❌ ➔ too poor ✅
  • comparatively safer ❌ ➔ comparatively safe ✅
  • relatively warmer ❌ ➔ relatively warm ✅
  • twice heavier than ❌ ➔ twice as heavy as ✅

Redundancy Traps:

  • "Adequate enough" ➔ Superfluous! Use either adequate OR enough.
  • "Equally as...as" ➔ Superfluous! Use either equally OR as...as.

🧩 4. The 'If Not' Combination Formula

When combining two comparative standards using 'if not':

As + Positive Degree + As + IF NOT + Comparative Degree
  • Wrong She is as beautiful if not beautiful than her sister.
  • Correct She is as beautiful as, if not more beautiful than, her sister.
  • Wrong He runs as fast if not fast than my friend.
  • Correct He runs as fast as, if not faster than, my friend.

9. OSASCOMP Order of Adjectives & Special Positions

Pages 12, 13 & 14 • Multiple Adjectives Ordering; Predicative-Only List; The + Adjective Plural

🎨 1. The OSASCOMP Order Rule

When multiple adjectives qualify the same noun, they MUST follow this standard order:

D: Determiner (A, the, two) O: Opinion (Beautiful, costly) S: Size (Large, big, tall) A: Age (Old, young, antique) S: Shape (Round, square) C: Colour (Black, yellow) O: Origin (Indian, Italian) M: Material (Wooden, leather) P: Purpose (Dining, wedding)
Master Example (Page 12):
"She purchased an expensive (Opinion), large (Size), antique (Age), round (Shape), yellow (Colour), Italian (Origin), wooden (Material), dining (Purpose) table."

👥 2. "The + Adjective = Plural Noun" Rule

Plural Collective Meaning

When 'The' is placed before certain qualitative adjectives, it converts the adjective into a PLURAL NOUN denoting the entire class!

  • The poor = Poor people
  • The rich = Rich people
  • The blind = Blind people
  • The sick = Sick people
  • The honest = Honest people

Strict Exam Rules:

  • Always takes a PLURAL VERB: The poor are hated by the rich.
  • NEVER add -s: The poors ❌, The blinds ❌.
  • NEVER add 'people': The poor people is redundant with The.
  • Note: Riches (Noun) means assets/wealth: Riches have wings.

🚫 3. Predicative-Only Adjectives (Page 14)

These adjectives can NEVER be used attributively (directly before a noun); they are used ONLY predicatively (after a linking verb):

Afraid Asleep Awake Alone Alike Alive Ablaze Afloat Ashamed Glad Fond Well Ill

• an afraid man ❌ ➔ a man who is afraid / a frightened man ✅.

• alive creatures ❌ ➔ living creatures / creatures that are alive ✅.

• a glad person ❌ ➔ a happy person / a person who is glad ✅.

• an asleep child ❌ ➔ a sleeping child ✅.

👃 4. Verbs of Sensation (Linking Verbs)

Verbs expressing sensory perception: Feel, Taste, Smell, Sound, Look, Seem, Appear, Become, Turn, Get, Grow, Keep, Make, Prove

➔ Must be followed by an ADJECTIVE (as complement), NOT an Adverb!

  • This fruit smells sweetly ❌ ➔ smells sweet ✅.
  • He feels badly ❌ ➔ feels bad ✅.
  • She looked happily ❌ ➔ looked happy ✅.
